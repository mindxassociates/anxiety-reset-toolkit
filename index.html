Ride the wave without feeding the alarm

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort. It typically reaches its strongest point within minutes. The sensations are real; after medical causes have been assessed, the sensations themselves can be approached as a powerful but temporary alarm response.

Map one of your loops

Fear vs. anxiety

Fear responds to danger happening now. Anxiety often predicts what might happen. First ask: “Is immediate action needed?”

Worry vs. planning

Planning ends in a specific next step. Worry repeats questions without new information. Try: “Is there an action I can take today?”

Why control backfires

Fighting thoughts and sensations keeps attention locked on them. Make room, observe, and choose behavior by your values.

A flexible response

Four moves for an anxiety wave

Move through these in order, or return to the move you need. Success means responding differently—not making every sensation disappear.

1

Name it

“My alarm system is active.” Add distance: “I’m noticing the thought that…”

2

Make room

Drop the argument. “This can be here for now.” Soften around the sensation.

3

Lean in safely

With curiosity, study the wave: Where? How strong? Moving or still? Do not provoke symptoms if medically unsafe.

4

Re-engage

Return attention to what matters: the conversation, task, drive, meal, or caring action.

Body sensations

A strong alarm can still be a false alarm.

Stress can change heart rate, breathing, digestion, balance, temperature, muscle tension, and perception. Interpretation often creates the “second wave”: fear of the sensation itself.

First wave

A sensation arrives: racing heart, dizziness, tightness, nausea, unreality, shaking.

Second wave

“This is dangerous.” Checking and escape amplify arousal and narrow attention.

New learning

Notice, allow, and stay—when safe—until you learn that discomfort rises, changes, and falls.

Racing heart or chest sensations

Try: “My body is mobilizing. I can let it beat without monitoring every beat.” Reduce pulse-checking. Seek urgent medical care for new or concerning chest pain, fainting, or symptoms that may be medical.

Shortness of breath or throat tightness

Let breathing regulate itself; avoid repeated “test breaths.” Relax the jaw and extend the exhale gently. New or severe breathing difficulty needs medical attention.

Dizziness, nausea, shaking, or unreality

Orient to the room, feel contact points, and describe the sensation as changing physical data. Allow natural shaking. Avoid repeatedly testing whether it is gone.

Intrusive or frightening thoughts

A thought is not an intention, prediction, or identity. Label it “a mental event,” let it pass without debating or neutralizing it, and rejoin your activity. If you may act on thoughts of harm, seek immediate help.

Therapist-guided skill

Learning not to fear body sensations

Interoceptive exposure deliberately creates a familiar, safe body sensation so the brain can test catastrophic predictions. It is a core element of CBT for panic—not a quick calming trick.

Examples to discuss with a therapist

Briefly running in place for a faster heartbeat, gently turning to notice dizziness, or breathing through a narrow straw to notice air hunger.

The learning questions

What did I predict? What sensation appeared? Did the feared outcome occur? Could I allow the sensation without checking, escaping, or neutralizing it?

Do not start these exercises on your own if you have a heart, lung, neurological, balance, pregnancy-related, or other relevant medical concern—or if you are unsure. Use exercises selected and screened by a qualified clinician. Never restrict breathing to a dangerous degree.

Clinical note: CBT for panic targets catastrophic interpretations and avoidance through real-life and body-sensation exposure. Repetition and reduction of safety behaviors help create new learning; the aim is increased flexibility, not forcing distress to fall during every exercise.

Build new learning

Approach in small, repeatable steps.

Avoidance shrinks life. Safe, planned practice teaches: “I can feel this and keep going.” Start challenging but doable; repeat before moving up.

Before practicing: Exposure is for situations that are uncomfortable but objectively safe. Do not use it for real danger, unsafe relationships, medical restrictions, intoxication, or activities requiring alertness when severely distressed. A therapist can help tailor the plan.

My practice ladder

Rate expected anxiety from 0–10. Add up to five steps, easiest first.

During practice

Stay long enough to gather new information.
Allow sensations instead of fighting them.
Notice what actually happens, not only what fear predicts.

Reduce safety behaviors

Gradually reduce unnecessary checking, escape plans, reassurance, distraction, carrying “just-in-case” items, or only going with a safe person.

Measure learning

Ask: What did I predict? What happened? What did I learn about my ability to cope? Progress is participation, not zero anxiety.

Keep it going

A five-minute daily reset

Small repetitions matter more than an occasional heroic effort.

1

Notice

Pause. Name thoughts, emotions, and sensations without fixing.

2

Get curious

Ask, “What does this feel like right now?” Use simple labels: tight, warm, pulsing, restless.

3

Check the payoff

Does the usual response truly help—or mainly give brief relief followed by a cost?

4

Offer kindness

“This is hard. Many humans know this feeling. What would support me?”

5

Choose one action

Take a small step toward health, connection, contribution, learning, or rest.

Foundations

Regular sleep timing, movement, food, social connection, and mindful use of caffeine/alcohol can lower vulnerability. Don’t turn them into perfection tests.
